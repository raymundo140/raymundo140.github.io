## Overview

At AIST (National Institute of Advanced Industrial Science and Technology) in Tsukuba, Japan, I worked as a Robotics Research Intern focused on designing and implementing a fully autonomous warehouse restocking robot system. The project centered on CALL-M < a custom mobile manipulator robot built at AIST > and spanned the full technical stack, from simulation infrastructure and sensor fusion through navigation, exploration, and high-level task coordination.

The project integrated a rich sensor suite; dual RPLidar A1M8 units for 360° environment scanning, two ZED Mini stereo cameras and an Intel RealSense D435i mounted on a Universal Robots UR5e arm for depth perception and visual processing, and four ultrasonic sonar sensors for close-range proximity detection. The UR5e arm played a dual role: it served as the manipulation platform for grasping and placement tasks. And its full 6-DOF articulation was actively exploited during mapping, using efficient motion planning algorithms to sweep the Intel RealSense through carefully computed trajectories, capturing depth data from angles and heights the mobile base and fixed-mount camera could not achieve alone. This allowed CALL-M to map occluded spaces, the tops and interiors of shelving units, and narrow areas inaccessible to the base, producing a significantly richer and more geometrically complete 3D map than what a fixed-mount sensor configuration would yield. All sensor streams were fused into a unified perception pipeline that applied reinforcement learning for autonomous exploration, deep learning for grasp pose estimation, computer vision for object and shelf detection, QR-based semantic identification, and full motion planning with MoveIt 2, running across an Intel NUC and an NVIDIA Jetson in a distributed ROS 2 architecture.

In parallel, I collaborated as co-author on a research paper tackling one of the classic open problems in mobile manipulation: autonomous door opening. The work involved detecting and localizing door handles using RGB-D perception, computing precise approach and grasp strategies under real-world uncertainty, and coordinating the UR5e arm and the omnidirectional base in a tightly coupled motion sequence to actuate the handle and push the door open.

This internship was an intense and rewarding learning experience. Working across the full robotics stack challenged me to grow technically in areas ranging from low-level sensor integration and ROS 2 architecture to machine learning and manipulation planning, while also developing my ability to manage complex, long-horizon engineering projects independently.

## The Robot: CALL-M

CALL-M is a research-grade mobile manipulator I worked with daily. Understanding its hardware deeply was essential to designing a robust software architecture around its capabilities and constraints.

### Hardware Architecture

| Subsystem | Component | Role |
|-----------|-----------|------|
| Mobile Base | TriOrb omnidirectional platform | Holonomic motion — moves in any direction without rotating |
| Arm | Universal Robots UR5e (6-DOF) | Manipulation, grasping, product placement |
| Gripper | Robotiq 2F-85 | Adaptive parallel gripper for box and product grasping |
| Lidar | 2× RPLidar A1M8 | 360° 2D laser scanning for mapping and obstacle detection |
| Stereo Cameras | 2× ZED Mini | Depth estimation and visual perception, mounted on Dynamixel servo motors |
| Depth Camera | Intel RealSense D435i | RGB-D perception for wrist-mounted manipulation sensing |
| Sonar | 4× MaxBotix MB1403 | Ultrasonic proximity sensing (front, back, left, right) |
| Main Computer | Intel NUC13 | Navigation, planning, ROS 2 control (ROS_DOMAIN_ID=0) |
| Perception Computer | NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX | ZED camera processing and visual inference |

The TriOrb base is a particularly interesting platform. Its three omnidirectional wheels arranged at 120° intervals allow the robot to translate in any direction and rotate simultaneously — a property called holonomic motion. This makes it significantly more agile than differential-drive robots in tight indoor spaces, but it also introduces complexity in the motion model that Nav2's default configurations do not handle out of the box. I had to carefully tune the velocity and acceleration constraints to match the physical capabilities of the platform.

The ZED Mini cameras are mounted on Dynamixel servo motors on the left and right sides of the base. This was a design choice I leveraged in my exploration algorithm — the servos can tilt the cameras toward the direction the robot is about to explore, maximizing depth coverage of the upcoming area before the robot even moves there.

The Intel RealSense D435i sits at the wrist of the UR5e arm, tilted 24° downward. At the arm's home configuration this places the camera at approximately 0.98m above the floor, giving an optimal viewing angle for detecting objects on shelves and on the ground.

## Simulation Infrastructure

Before working on the real robot, I built a complete high-fidelity simulation environment in Gazebo Fortress (Ignition) with ROS 2 Humble. This was not a superficial simulation — every sensor, joint, and controller present on the real robot is replicated in simulation with accurate physical parameters.

### Why Simulation First

Developing autonomy directly on the real robot carries risk — failed navigation goals can cause collisions, incorrect sensor configurations waste hours of real hardware time, and bugs in the exploration algorithm could drive the robot into walls. Building the complete simulation first allowed me to iterate the full pipeline at high speed, catch architectural mistakes early, and arrive at the real robot with a system that had already been validated end-to-end.

### Simulation Stack

The simulation uses Gazebo Fortress as the physics engine, with `ros_gz_bridge` (parameter bridge) providing bidirectional topic translation between Gazebo's internal messaging and ROS 2. I authored a modular xacro-based URDF description that separates the robot's physical model from its simulation-specific plugins, allowing the same description files to be used for both simulation and real robot deployment with a single `use_sim` flag.

Key simulation components I implemented:

- **Lidar bridge**: Translates Gazebo `LaserScan` messages to ROS 2 `sensor_msgs/LaserScan` on `/call_m/scan/front` and `/call_m/scan/back`
- **Depth camera bridge**: Bridges the RealSense depth image and pointcloud from Gazebo to ROS 2 on `/call_m/call_m_camera/camera/depth/image_raw/points`
- **ZED camera bridge**: Bridges both ZED Mini cameras' RGB and depth streams
- **Sonar bridge**: Translates 4× sonar LaserScan plugins to `sensor_msgs/Range` messages
- **Odometry bridge**: Bridges the TriOrb wheel odometry to `/call_m/odom`
- **ros2_control integration**: All joints (3 TriOrb wheels, 6 UR5e arm joints, 2 ZED servo joints) are controlled through `gz_ros2_control` with position and velocity interfaces
- **TF remapper**: A custom node that handles the namespace remapping needed to correctly resolve TF frames across the `call_m/` namespace prefix

The simulation launches in three modes depending on the task:

```
mode:=sim_only   — Gazebo + sensors + controllers (lightest, used for mapping)
mode:=sim_fox    — + Foxglove bridge on port 8765 (default for visualization)
mode:=sim_full   — + MoveIt2 + RViz (used for manipulation development)
```

### Warehouse World

I constructed a custom Gazebo world representing an indoor warehouse/convenience store environment with shelving units, corridors, open floor areas, and static obstacles. The world is sized to exercise the full range of the exploration algorithm — large enough that the robot must make hundreds of navigation decisions to fully map it, but constrained enough that mapping completes in a reasonable simulation time.

## Phase 1: Autonomous Mapping

### Goal

Enable CALL-M to autonomously explore a completely unknown indoor environment, build a geometrically accurate 2D occupancy grid map, and save it to disk — with zero human teleoperation.

### The 5-Node Pipeline

I designed Phase 1 as a five-node processing pipeline where each node has a single, well-defined responsibility. Data flows from raw sensors through successive layers of abstraction until it reaches the map and the navigation stack.

```
Raw Sensors
    │
    ▼
Node 1 — Unified Scan Builder      /call_m/unified_scan
    │
    ▼
Node 2 — Dynamic Obstacle Filter   /call_m/static_scan
    │                              /call_m/dynamic_scan
    ├──────────────────────────────────────┐
    ▼                                      ▼
Node 3 — SLAM Toolbox              Node 4 — Nav2
    │          /map                        │     /call_m/cmd_vel
    └──────────────────────────────────────┘
                                           ▲
                                    Node 5 — Exploration Manager
                                           /exploration/goal
```

---

### Node 1 — Unified Scan Builder

The most technically complex node in the pipeline. Its job is to take heterogeneous sensor data from five different sensor types — two lidars, four sonars, two ZED depth cameras, and one Intel RealSense — and unify them into a single coherent 360° LaserScan.

#### Why sensor fusion matters

Each sensor on CALL-M has different strengths and blind spots. The RPLidar A1M8 units provide long-range 2D scans but have no vertical resolution — they cannot detect obstacles above or below their scan plane. The ZED Mini cameras provide rich depth information in a wide FOV but have a limited range and cannot see directly to the sides of the robot. The sonars provide reliable close-range detection in their respective directions but with very coarse angular resolution (58° beam width). The Intel RealSense provides the highest-quality RGB-D data but faces downward from the wrist and covers a small forward arc.

Fusing all of these into one unified scan gives the navigation stack a complete, redundant picture of the robot's surroundings that no single sensor could provide alone.

#### Sub-node architecture

Node 1 is composed of six sub-nodes running in parallel:

**`zed_depth_to_pointcloud` (×2)** — I wrote this node from scratch. The ZED Mini cameras publish depth images as `sensor_msgs/Image` where each pixel is a float32 depth value in meters. The navigation stack needs `sensor_msgs/PointCloud2`. My node performs the back-projection calculation:

```
x = (u - cx) / fx * z
y = (v - cy) / fy * z
```

where `(cx, cy)` is the camera principal point and `(fx, fy)` are the focal lengths computed from the camera's field of view. I use NumPy to perform this computation on all 230,400 pixels simultaneously rather than looping, keeping processing time under 5ms per frame. Invalid depths (NaN, below 0.3m, above 8.0m) are filtered before packing the result into a PointCloud2 message.

**`sonar_to_scan`** — Another node I wrote from scratch. The MaxBotix MB1403 sonar sensors publish `sensor_msgs/Range` — a single distance measurement with a 58° cone beam width. The laser scan merger expects `sensor_msgs/LaserScan`. My conversion spreads the single distance reading across 58 rays (1° resolution) centered on the sonar's mounting direction, correctly representing the sonar's angular uncertainty. Invalid readings (outside 0.30m–5.00m range) are set to infinity rather than discarded, correctly signaling "no obstacle detected" rather than introducing false data.

**`depth_filter_scan_converter` (×3)** — This is an external package (Ocean's `depth-filter-scan-converter`) that takes a PointCloud2 and produces a LaserScan slice at a configurable height band above the floor. I configured three instances — one for the Intel RealSense and one for each ZED Mini — each with carefully tuned parameters:

- `height_offset: 0.06m` — the TriOrb base height above the floor, measured experimentally from odometry
- `min_height: 0.05m` — ignore floor reflections
- `max_height: 1.50m` — capture everything up to the robot's maximum clearance height
- `cliff_detect: true` — detect floor holes and steps as obstacles

**`laserscan_toolbox_node`** — Another Ocean package (`multi-laserscan-toolbox-ros2`) that merges all individual LaserScan inputs into a single unified scan using odometry interpolation to correct for the robot's motion between scan timestamps. I configured it to merge: 2 lidar scans + 4 sonar scans + 3 depth-converted scans = 9 input streams into one `/call_m/unified_scan`.

---

### Node 2 — Dynamic Obstacle Filter

Node 2 receives the unified scan and splits it into two output streams:

- `/call_m/static_scan` → fed to SLAM Toolbox (only permanent obstacles should influence the map)
- `/call_m/dynamic_scan` → fed to Nav2's local costmap (moving obstacles influence navigation but not the map)

For Phase 1, the dynamic filter operates in **template mode** — all scan data passes to static_scan while the classification algorithm is being developed. The architectural separation is in place so that when the real dynamic filtering is implemented in Phase 2, no other part of the pipeline needs to change.

---

### Node 3 — SLAM Toolbox

SLAM Toolbox runs in asynchronous mapping mode, subscribing to `/call_m/static_scan` and building a 2D occupancy grid map in the `map` frame. Key configuration decisions I made:

- **Solver**: `CeresSolver` with `SPARSE_NORMAL_CHOLESKY` linear solver and `SCHUR_JACOBI` preconditioner — the best performance trade-off for the map size
- **Scan topic**: `/call_m/static_scan` (not the raw lidar scan, so the depth cameras contribute to the map)
- **Mode**: `mapping` (not localization — we are building the map, not localizing within a known one)
- **odom_frame**: `call_m/odom` — matching the namespaced TF frame published by the TriOrb odometry
- **Resolution**: 0.05m per cell — sufficient for navigation while keeping memory usage manageable
- **Loop closure**: enabled with chain size 10, allowing the robot to correct accumulated drift when it revisits previously mapped areas

---

### Node 4 — Nav2

The full Nav2 navigation stack handles path planning and obstacle avoidance. I configured it with:

- **Global planner**: NavFn (Dijkstra-based global path planning on the occupancy grid)
- **Local controller**: DWB (Dynamic Window Approach) with critics for rotation-to-goal, oscillation suppression, base obstacle avoidance, goal alignment, path alignment, path distance, and goal distance
- **Global costmap**: static layer (from `/map`) + obstacle layer (from `/call_m/static_scan`)
- **Local costmap**: static layer + voxel layer (from `/call_m/dynamic_scan`)
- **Robot footprint**: circular approximation of the TriOrb base radius (0.37m)
- **Transform tolerance**: 0.2s — to handle timing jitter between the simulation clock and node processing

The costmap setup ensures that static obstacles (walls, shelving) influence both global path planning and local avoidance, while dynamic obstacles (people, moving boxes) only affect local avoidance without contaminating the map.

### Node 5 — Exploration Manager

The exploration manager is the primary research contribution of Phase 1 and the component I am investing the most effort into. Its job is to make autonomous decisions about where the robot should go next in order to map the environment as efficiently as possible.

My approach followed a deliberate two-stage research methodology: first, I built a classical heuristic baseline to get the full pipeline working end-to-end and generate reference data; then, I worked on replacing the heuristic decision-maker with a reinforcement learning policy trained against that baseline. This structure was intentional — a working baseline is a prerequisite for meaningful RL training, and the performance gap between the two approaches is itself a measurable research result.

#### Stage 1 — Classical Baseline: FFD + Information Gain Scoring

The baseline exploration policy uses Fast Frontier Detection (FFD) combined with an Information Gain / Travel Cost scoring function. A frontier is defined as a free cell (occupancy value 0) adjacent to at least one unknown cell (occupancy value -1) — representing the boundary between explored and unexplored space.

My baseline implementation:
1. Parses the current `/map` occupancy grid as a NumPy array
2. Detects all frontier cells via 8-connected neighborhood checks
3. Clusters adjacent frontier cells into frontier regions using BFS
4. Filters clusters smaller than 3 cells (noise rejection)
5. Scores each cluster as `score = information_gain / travel_cost`
6. Selects the highest-scoring frontier and sends it to Nav2 as a navigation goal

Information gain is estimated by ray casting 36 rays (every 10°, up to 8m) from each frontier centroid and counting unknown cells encountered before hitting an obstacle. This provides a fast, geometry-based approximation of how much new area the robot would reveal by visiting each candidate.

The baseline is fully functional and serves two purposes: it validates the complete pipeline end-to-end, and it generates the exploration trajectory data — map states, frontier sets, decisions made, coverage achieved — that I use to train and evaluate the RL policy.

#### Stage 2 — Research Contribution: Reinforcement Learning Exploration Policy

The central research question I investigated was whether a learned exploration policy can outperform the classical heuristic in terms of mapping efficiency — specifically: *does the robot cover more area per unit of travel distance when exploration decisions are made by a trained policy rather than a hand-crafted scoring function?*

I framed this as a Markov Decision Process (MDP):

- **State**: a local occupancy grid patch centered on the robot, current frontier set with positions and sizes, robot velocity, and elapsed mission time
- **Action**: selection of the next frontier goal from the detected candidate set
- **Reward**: positive reward proportional to new cells mapped since the last decision, negative reward proportional to distance traveled, penalty for blacklisted goals and stuck states
- **Episode**: one complete exploration run from an empty map to full coverage or timeout

The policy was trained entirely in simulation using the Gazebo environment I built for Phase 1, which allowed thousands of training episodes to be run without real hardware time. The trained policy was deployed as a drop-in replacement for the heuristic scorer inside the exploration manager node — the rest of the pipeline (Nav2, SLAM Toolbox, sensor fusion) remained identical, making the comparison between baseline and RL policy clean and controlled.

The RL integration was designed so that the node could switch between the heuristic baseline and the learned policy at launch time via a single parameter, enabling direct A/B comparison in the same environment.

#### ZED Servo Integration (Novel Contribution)

Regardless of which exploration policy is active, when a frontier is selected I command the ZED servo motors to pre-tilt the cameras toward the direction of the selected frontier before the robot begins moving. The tilt angle is computed from the frontier distance:

```python
tilt_rad = atan2(camera_height, distance)
```

This pre-positioned the depth cameras to maximize coverage of the area the robot was about to enter — capturing obstacle data ahead of arrival rather than only after. This was a hardware-specific contribution enabled by CALL-M's servo-mounted cameras that has no equivalent on robots with fixed sensor placement.

#### Blacklisting and Recovery

Frontiers that Nav2 could not reach or that timed out after 120 seconds were blacklisted for 60 seconds, preventing the robot from repeatedly attempting unreachable goals. This applied to both the heuristic and RL policies. If all frontiers were blacklisted the system entered STUCK status and waited for the map to update with new candidates.

#### Visualization

During exploration I publish:
- `/exploration/frontiers` as `visualization_msgs/MarkerArray` — green sphere marks the currently selected frontier, blue spheres mark all other candidates
- `/exploration/goal` as `geometry_msgs/PoseStamped` — the active Nav2 goal pose
- `/exploration/status` as `std_msgs/String` — current state (EXPLORING / COMPLETE / STUCK / PAUSED / WAITING_FOR_MAP)

These feed directly into Foxglove for real-time monitoring of exploration progress.

## Phase 2: Autonomous Restocking Pipeline

*(Architecture designed during the internship; implementation was in progress at the time my internship concluded.)*

Phase 2 uses the map produced by Phase 1, enriched with semantic annotations marking supply zones, shelf locations, and return areas.

### Pipeline

1. **Navigate to supply zone** — Using Nav2 with the saved map for global localization (AMCL)
2. **QR code identification** — The Intel RealSense captures the QR code on each box. A custom ROS 2 node decodes the QR and queries a product database to determine which shelf the box belongs to
3. **RGB-D product detection** — The ZED Mini cameras verify the box contents and detect the target shelf position using depth-informed bounding box detection
4. **Navigate to target shelf** — Nav2 routes the robot through the environment to the correct shelf location
5. **Manipulation with MoveIt 2** — I use MoveIt 2 to plan and execute the arm trajectory for grasping the box, approaching the shelf, placing products, and returning the empty box
6. **Verification** — Post-placement verification using the RealSense confirms correct placement before the robot returns for the next box

### Recovery Strategies

The system handles four failure modes:

| Failure | Recovery Strategy |
|---------|-----------------|
| Dynamic obstacle blocks path | Nav2 replans around obstacle; if blocked >30s, request human intervention |
| QR code unreadable | Reposition robot, retry ×3; escalate to VLM for visual identification |
| Product detection failure | Retry with adjusted lighting compensation; flag for manual review |
| Grasp failure | Re-plan grasp pose with updated depth data; retry ×2 before aborting |

I evaluated Vision-Language Models (VLMs) as a potential fallback layer for cases where structured detection fails — using the VLM's semantic understanding to interpret ambiguous scenes and suggest the next action.

## Secondary Project: Autonomous Door Opening

I contributed as co-author to a research paper on autonomous door opening with CALL-M. This task required tight coordination between the arm and the mobile base — the arm had to grasp and turn the handle while the base simultaneously moved to push the door open without losing the grasp.

My contributions to this project included:

- RGB-D door and handle detection pipeline using the Intel RealSense
- Robot positioning strategy — computing the approach pose relative to the detected door frame
- MoveIt 2 motion planning for handle grasping, incorporating force compliance constraints to avoid breaking the handle or losing the grasp during rotation
- Base coordination — commanding the TriOrb base to move in synchrony with the arm motion as the door swings open

This project was one of the most mechanically complex manipulation tasks I worked on during the internship. See the linked research paper and project page below for the full results.
